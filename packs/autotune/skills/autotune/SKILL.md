# autotune

## Overview

Machine prose is rarely wrong. It is autotuned. Every note sits dead centre of
its pitch, and that is what gives it away.

**Overcorrecting fails too.** Leave the tells in and the piece reads as machine
work. Scrub every one of them out and it still does, because flat, opinion-free
prose is itself the loudest tell. A voice is partly the notes it misses on
purpose.

## Process

1. Read the house, so you can tell a habit from a fingerprint.
2. Fix the frequent tells: 13, 10, 9, 32, 33, 27.
3. Work the full list below.
4. Add soul.
5. Stop, using the rule at the end.

## First, read the house

Before changing anything, open the nearest few documents: other posts on the
blog, other pages on the site, the last few commits.

Anything the house does consistently is a voice, not a tell. A writer who uses
em dashes in every piece has a convention, and stripping them is not unslopping.
It is overwriting a person.

Only what the house does *not* otherwise do counts. Skip this and you will edit
the author into sounding like everyone else, which is the thing you were brought
in to prevent.

## The list

### Content

1. **Puffery.** "pivotal moment", "testament to", "evolving landscape", "setting the stage for", "indelible mark", "deeply rooted". Cut it, state what happened.
2. **Name-dropping.** Listing outlets or logos without context. Pick one, say what it actually said.
3. **Superficial -ing clauses.** "highlighting...", "ensuring...", "reflecting...", "showcasing...", "fostering...". Delete, or expand into a real claim.
4. **Promotional language.** "nestled", "vibrant", "breathtaking", "groundbreaking", "renowned", "stunning", "must-visit". Describe neutrally.
5. **Vague attributions.** "Experts believe", "industry reports suggest", "some critics argue". Name the source or drop the claim.
6. **Formulaic challenges.** "Despite challenges, X continues to thrive." Replace with the specific fact.

### Language

7. **AI vocabulary.** Additionally, crucial, delve, enduring, enhance, fostering, garner, interplay, intricate, landscape (abstract), pivotal, showcase, tapestry, testament, underscore, vibrant. Use the plain word.
8. **Fancy ways to say "is".** "serves as", "stands as", "boasts", "features". Say "is" or "has".
9. **"Not just X, but Y."** Also "it's not about X, it's about Y". State the point once.
10. **Rule of three.** Three examples, three adjectives, three clauses, when the real number was two or five. Count what you have and use that.
11. **Synonym cycling.** Protagonist, main character, central figure, hero in one paragraph. Pick one and repeat it.
12. **False ranges.** "from X to Y" where X and Y are not on a scale. List the things.

### Style

13. **Em dashes.** Unless the house uses them. End the sentence or use a comma. Reaching for parentheses instead trades one tell for another.
14. **Colons mid-sentence.** Fine before a list or example, not as a connector between two clauses.
15. **Boldface.** Don't bold every proper noun and acronym.
16. **Inline-header lists.** The tell is a bold label plus colon restating the line: "**Performance:** Performance improved". A bold lead-in ending in a period, followed by new detail, is fine.
17. **Title case headings.** Sentence case.
18. **Decorative emoji.** Remove from headings and bullets.
19. **Curly quotes.** Straight quotes.

### Communication artifacts

20. **Chatbot phrases.** "I hope this helps", "let me know if", "of course", "certainly", "great question".
21. **Cutoff disclaimers.** "While specific details are limited..." Find the detail or cut the sentence.
22. **Sycophancy.** "You're absolutely right." Answer directly.

### Filler

23. **Filler phrases.** "In order to" to "to". "Due to the fact that" to "because". "It is important to note that" deleted outright.
24. **Stacked hedging.** "could potentially possibly be argued that it might" to "may".
25. **Generic conclusions.** "The future looks bright." State the plan or the number.

### Jargon

26. **Abstract metaphor nouns.** Substrate, wedge, vector, locus, nexus, primitive, harness, surface, bedrock, scaffolding, paradigm, ratchet, endgame, north star, flywheel. Substrate is a base. A wedge is an addition. A vector is a method. Pick the concrete word.

### Plain speech

27. **Fluent nothing.** Sentences that would sit unchanged in a competitor's docs. Restate each as an instruction, a fact, or a number. If it cannot be restated, it was never saying anything.
28. **Dense sentences.** If the reader backtracks to parse it, split it. One idea per sentence.
29. **Passive voice.** "Queries are validated" to "the compiler validates queries". Passive is fine only when the actor genuinely does not matter.
30. **Adverbs.** "Runs quickly" to "is fast", or to the number. An adverb propping up a weak verb means the verb is wrong.
31. **Fancy synonyms.** Utilize to use. Leverage to use. Facilitate to help. Numerous to many.

### Rhythm and shape

32. **Even rhythm.** Sentence lengths clustered between fifteen and twenty-five words with nothing short. Write the lengths out as a list of numbers and look at it. Then break one in half.
33. **The summarising close.** A final paragraph restating what was already said. Cut it. Writing ends when the point lands, not when the shape feels complete.

## Adding soul

Removing tells is half the job. Voiceless writing is just as obvious.

- **Have an opinion.** React to the facts instead of listing them evenly.
- **Vary the rhythm.** Short sentences. Then longer ones that take their time.
- **Admit complexity.** "Impressive, and slightly unsettling" beats "impressive".
- **Use "I" where it fits.** First person is not unprofessional.
- **Let some mess in.** Perfect structure looks machine-made.
- **Be specific.** Not "this is concerning" but "agents churning away at 3am".

## The stopping rule

Read the result and answer one question: **whose is this?**

If the answer is "someone careful" rather than a named person, you went too far.
Put back something only the author would write: a sentence that runs long
because they were enjoying themselves, or a word they overuse.

A piece can carry three or four tells and still read as human. A piece with none
and no personality reads as a press release.

## Output contract

1. **The tells found.** A table: the phrase, its number from the list, and
   whether the house does it elsewhere. Write this before editing, or you will
   justify whatever you already changed.
2. **The rewrite.** Same length or shorter.
3. **The count.** Tells removed, tells kept on purpose, and why.

## Leave alone

- Quotes, testimonials, anything in someone else's voice
- Technical terms of art where the reader is technical
- Any pattern the house uses consistently, however much you dislike it
- The argument. If the piece is wrong, say so separately

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Running it without reading the house first | You cannot tell a habit from a fingerprint with no baseline. Every judgement after that is a guess. |
| Treating the list as a checklist to zero | Zero tells and zero voice is the failure this skill exists to avoid. |
| Swapping a flagged word for a fancier one | "Delve" to "explore" is lateral. "Delve into" to "read" is the fix. |
| Rewriting first, listing second | The list stops being evidence and becomes an alibi. |
| Applying it to someone's personal essay | Voice-driven writing gets to break these on purpose. |
