# buzzkill

## Overview

Corporate register is not a vocabulary problem. It is a way of avoiding
commitment. Every buzzword marks a specific claim the writer declined to make,
and swapping it for a synonym leaves the evasion intact.

**The fix is never a better word. It is asking what the sentence would have to
say if it had to be true.**

## The test that decides everything

**The inverse test.** Take any claim. Would a competitor ever assert the
opposite?

No company says "our platform is unreliable and slow." So "reliable and fast" is
not a claim. It is noise wearing the costume of a claim. Cut it, or replace it
with the number that would let a reader disagree.

Apply this before any rewriting. Most of a bad draft dies here.

## The five passes

Run in order. Each pass reads the whole draft looking for one thing only.
Mixing them means missing things.

**1. Abstraction.** Nouns that name no object: *solutions, capabilities,
offerings, innovation, transformation, journey, ecosystem, experiences.* For
each, ask "what is the physical thing or observable event?" Replace with that.
If there isn't one, the sentence has no content and goes.

**2. Agentless.** Sentences where nobody does anything: *efficiencies were
realized, the migration was completed, value is delivered.* Rewrite as
subject-verb-object. Who did what to whom? If the writer is hiding the actor,
find out why. Usually the actor is unflattering, or nobody actually did it.

**3. Unfalsifiable.** *Industry-leading, best-in-class, seamless, robust,
world-class, cutting-edge, enterprise-grade, next-generation.* Attach a number,
a comparison, or a named alternative. Otherwise cut it. Never rewrite one into
a different adjective.

**4. Hedges.** *Helps, can, designed to, aims to, up to, may, enables you to.*
Each hedge is a claim the writer wasn't confident enough to make. Two options
only: commit to it, or cut it. "Helps reduce costs" becomes "cut our bill by
40%" or disappears.

**5. Register.** *Leverage → use. Utilize → use. Facilitate → help. Enable →
let. Deliver → give/send/do. Drive → cause. Unlock → get.* Mechanical, do it
last, and don't let it feel like the job is done. It's the smallest pass.

## Output contract

Return two things, in this order:

1. **The tells.** A table with the original phrase, which pass caught it, and
   what it was avoiding saying. One row per tell. This is the part with value;
   it teaches the writer to catch their own.
2. **The rewrite.** The full draft, revised. Same length or shorter. Never
   longer.

Report the before/after word count. A real buzzkill pass usually cuts 20-40%.

## Don't touch

- Quotes and testimonials. A person's actual words, even bad ones
- Legal, regulatory, or compliance-required language
- Technical terms of art, where the reader is technical
- Product and feature names, however bad
- The writer's argument. Register is the target. If you disagree with the point,
  say so separately rather than editing it away.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Replacing a buzzword with a nicer buzzword | The evasion survives. Ask what it was avoiding. |
| Rewriting first, listing tells second | The list is the deliverable. Write it first or you'll rationalize your edits. |
| Making it longer to make it specific | Specificity is shorter. "Enterprise-grade security" → "SOC 2 Type II". |
| Cutting hedges from genuinely uncertain claims | If it's actually uncertain, say what's uncertain and why. Hedging isn't the same as honesty. |
| Applying it to a personal essay | This is for marketing and corporate copy. Voice-driven writing has different rules. |
